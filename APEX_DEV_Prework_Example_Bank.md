# APEX DEV — Pre-work example bank

Use this file as the selectable example list for **Your problem**. Show `title` in the selection UI. When a participant chooses an example, load only `problem_statement` into the editable problem field. Participants may keep, revise, replace, or write a completely new problem. Do not load app ideas or proposed features with an example.

Each statement is a complete sentence, ready to import. Its problem is a concrete difficulty observed in school; it does not prescribe a web app as the solution or limit the technology used to build one. The examples work as starting points for a small first version. Use sample or anonymized data where individual records would be sensitive.

## Examples

### 01. Attendance at a glance

**Problem statement:** Teachers who teach several classes struggle to see who is absent and total attendance promptly when starting a lesson because attendance marks are recorded in separate paper lists that take time to locate and count.

### 02. Assignment submissions

**Problem statement:** Teachers who collect handwritten assignments struggle to see which students still need to submit their work when checking several assignments over a week because submission notes are kept on separate sheets and are easy to overlook.

### 03. Consent form status

**Problem statement:** Teachers who collect permission forms for school activities struggle to identify which forms are still missing when preparing for an event because different forms are returned on different days and their submission status is recorded in several places.

### 04. Work missed during absence

**Problem statement:** Teachers who help absent students catch up struggle to remember which activities each student needs to complete when students return on different days because missed tasks are tracked through loose notes and memory.

### 05. Lesson plan reuse

**Problem statement:** Teachers who teach the same topic to different classes struggle to find and adapt a lesson plan they have used before when preparing another lesson because their plans are stored in several notebooks or files without a consistent way to find them.

### 06. Reusable review questions

**Problem statement:** Teachers who prepare short review activities struggle to reuse questions they have already written when revisiting a topic because previous questions are scattered across notebooks and worksheets.

### 07. Group formation

**Problem statement:** Teachers who organize group activities struggle to create new groups quickly when starting an activity because rearranging a class list by hand takes time and often produces the same groups.

### 08. Seating arrangements

**Problem statement:** Teachers who adjust classroom seating struggle to compare possible arrangements when students' learning needs or classroom dynamics change because moving names on a paper chart and redrawing alternatives takes time.

### 09. Participation rotation

**Problem statement:** Teachers who want more students to contribute struggle to keep track of who has already spoken when leading a class discussion because they must remember previous turns while listening and managing the lesson.

### 10. Weekly teaching schedule

**Problem statement:** Teachers who teach several classes struggle to check their own weekly schedule when planning materials for the next day because class times and room details are spread across different paper notices.

### 11. Curriculum progress

**Problem statement:** Teachers who follow a sequence of learning topics struggle to see what has been taught and what still needs attention when planning the coming weeks because progress notes are scattered across lesson plans and calendars.

### 12. Assessment dates

**Problem statement:** Teachers who set assessments for several classes struggle to see upcoming dates together when planning the next few weeks because dates are recorded separately for each class.

### 13. Weighted score calculation

**Problem statement:** Teachers who calculate assessment results struggle to apply the same weighting rules accurately to many sample scores when preparing a term summary because repeated calculations are done by hand.

### 14. Reading activity overview

**Problem statement:** Teachers who run regular reading activities struggle to see which kinds of books the class has explored when planning the next activity because titles and topics are recorded across separate classroom logs.

### 15. Classroom materials checklist

**Problem statement:** Teachers who prepare materials for hands-on lessons struggle to check whether everything is ready before class because supply needs are written in different lesson notes and are easy to miss.

### 16. School event task list

**Problem statement:** Teachers who help organize a school event struggle to keep track of their own preparation tasks when the event approaches because deadlines and materials are mentioned in several meetings and messages.

### 17. Meeting action items

**Problem statement:** School staff who attend planning meetings struggle to remember which follow-up tasks they personally agreed to complete when preparing for the next meeting because action items are buried in long meeting notes.

### 18. Shared-device practice

**Problem statement:** Teachers who run practice activities in a computer room struggle to give small groups a clear sequence of questions when students take turns at shared computers because printed question sheets can be misplaced or used out of order.

### 19. Self-paced practice cards

**Problem statement:** Students who use a school computer room for independent practice struggle to choose a suitable next question when finishing one task because the available practice sheets do not show a clear path from easier to harder questions.

### 20. Lesson exit reflections

**Problem statement:** Teachers who want to reflect on a lesson struggle to spot recurring questions from students when planning the next lesson because brief comments collected on paper are difficult to group and review quickly.

## Facilitator note

- Examples 01–17 and 20 lead naturally to a teacher or staff tool. Examples 18–19 allow supervised student use with shared computers; neither assumes one device per student.
- When discussing a prototype, use fictional names, sample scores, or anonymous counts. Do not ask participants to enter real student, parent, health, behavior, or consent records into a demo. Google Apps Script server functions and shared storage may be used when a participant's app needs them; decide this from the selected problem and app idea rather than restricting the reference problem.
- The selection UI should display short English titles, then let the participant preview and edit the full sentence. Choosing an example replaces only the problem sentence after a clear confirmation if it would overwrite the participant's own work.
